    History of Differential Equations

    The study of differential equations began in the 17th century with the independent discoveries of Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. Newton’s work on “fluxions” and Leibniz’s development of the differential notation dy/dx laid the groundwork for modern calculus. Early applications included problems of motion, growth, and decay. Over the centuries, mathematicians such as Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace expanded the theory, leading to the rich field of ordinary and partial differential equations that underpins much of physics, engineering, and applied mathematics today.

    By the 18th century, the systematic classification of ODEs (linear, separable, exact, homogeneous, etc.) emerged, thanks to the work of mathematicians like Jean le Rond d'Alembert and Joseph-Louis Lagrange. The 19th century saw the introduction of powerful solution techniques such as the method of integrating factors, series solutions, and the theory of Sturm‑Liouville eigenvalue problems. In the 20th century, the rise of functional analysis, numerical methods, and computer algebra systems transformed how we understand and solve differential equations, making them indispensable in modern science and engineering.
