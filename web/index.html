Welcome to Calculus

Calculus is the mathematical study of change. It has two fundamental branches:

  • Differentiation – deals with rates of change and slopes of curves.
  • Integration – deals with accumulation of quantities and areas under curves.

History

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) independently discovered calculus in the late 17th century, around the same time as Isaac Newton. Leibniz introduced the integral sign “∫” (derived from the Latin word “summa”) and the differential notation “dx”, which have become the standard symbols used worldwide. His work on the fundamental theorem of calculus linked differentiation and integration, laying the groundwork for modern analysis. The famous priority dispute between Leibniz and Newton sparked intense debate, but today both are credited with the invention of calculus.

Sir Isaac Newton (1642–1727) developed his own formulation of calculus, which he called the “method of fluxions”. Newton’s approach focused on rates of change (fluxions) and the accumulation of quantities (fluents), and he applied these ideas to solve problems in physics, such as planetary motion and the law of universal gravitation. Although Newton did not publish his calculus until later, his work laid the foundation for the analytical mechanics that would dominate the scientific revolution. Together with Leibniz’s notation, Newton’s concepts form the dual heritage of modern calculus.

Foundations and Further Developments

Beyond the pioneering work of Newton and Leibniz, many mathematicians contributed essential ideas that shaped modern calculus:

  • Archimedes (c. 287–212 BC) – used the method of exhaustion, an early form of limits, to find areas and volumes.
  • Johannes Kepler (1571–1630) – his laws of planetary motion motivated the need for a systematic study of rates of change.
  • Bonaventura Cavalieri (1598–1647) – developed the principle of indivisibles, a precursor to integral calculus.
  • Isaac Barrow (1630–1677) – proved the fundamental theorem of calculus in a geometric form, directly influencing Newton.
  • Augustin-Louis Cauchy (1789–1857) – formalized the definition of limits, continuity, and gave rigorous foundations to differentiation and integration.
  • Karl Weierstrass (1815–1897) – introduced the ε‑δ definition of limit, removing reliance on infinitesimals and providing a fully rigorous basis.
  • Bernhard Riemann (1826–1866) – created the Riemann integral, extending integration to a broader class of functions.
  • Georg Cantor (1845–1918) – developed set theory and the concept of cardinality, influencing measure theory and modern integration.
  • Henri Lebesgue (1875–1941) – introduced Lebesgue integration, further generalizing the integral and linking it with measure theory.

These contributions, together with the original insights of Newton and Leibniz, form the rich tapestry of calculus as it is taught and applied today.
